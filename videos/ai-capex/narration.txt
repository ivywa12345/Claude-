Imagine waking up one morning and hearing this:

Microsoft is cutting its AI spending.

Google is slowing down data-center construction.

Amazon is delaying new GPU purchases.

Meta is pushing back its next round of AI infrastructure investment.

And Nvidia suddenly tells investors:

“We still expect strong AI demand — but customers are no longer increasing their spending at the same pace.”

The first reaction would probably be obvious.

Sell Nvidia.

Sell semiconductors.

Sell data centers.

Sell anything related to AI.

But there is a much bigger question.

What happens to all the money that was supposed to be spent on AI?

Because this isn't just about Nvidia.

The AI boom has become one of the largest capital-spending cycles in modern history.

Hundreds of billions of dollars are being committed to GPUs, data centers, networking equipment, power plants, cooling systems and electricity infrastructure.

And if AI capex eventually peaks, or even collapses...

where does that capital go?

That question may be more important than whether AI is a bubble.

The AI Boom Is Really a Capital-Spending Boom.

When people talk about the AI boom, they usually talk about artificial intelligence.

But financially, something else is happening.

It's a massive capital-expenditure cycle.

The chain looks something like this:

Investors provide capital.

Big technology companies spend that capital.

They build data centers.

They buy GPUs.

They buy networking equipment.

They consume enormous amounts of electricity.

And companies like Nvidia, Broadcom, utilities, construction companies and data-center operators collect the money.

Then investors look at those companies and say:

“AI demand is exploding.”

Their valuations rise.

That makes it easier for them to raise even more capital.

And the cycle continues.

So the AI boom isn't just a technology story.

It's also a financial cycle.

And financial cycles eventually face one question:

What is the return on all this capital?

The Problem Isn't That AI Doesn't Work.

This is where I think the AI debate often gets confused.

There are two very different statements:

AI is transformative.

And:

Every dollar being invested in AI infrastructure today will earn an attractive return.

The first statement can be true while the second one is completely false.

Think about the early internet.

The internet was real.

It changed the world.

But investors still built too much fiber-optic capacity, too many networks and too many companies.

The technology won.

Many investors didn't.

That's an important distinction.

The AI economy could be exactly the same.

AI could become one of the most important technologies in history...

while the market still spends too much money, too quickly, on the wrong infrastructure.

So What Happens If AI Capex Peaks?

Let's say something extreme happens.

Today, hyperscalers are increasing AI infrastructure spending aggressively.

But over the next two or three years, something changes.

Companies realize:

“We've built enough capacity.”

AI models become more efficient.

Inference becomes cheaper.

GPU utilization improves.

And the economic return from another $100 billion of data centers isn't as attractive as it used to be.

So instead of increasing capex by 30 or 40 percent...

companies increase it by 5 percent.

Then zero.

And eventually...

capex starts falling.

Now let's ask the interesting question.

Where does the money go?

I see at least five possible destinations.

Destination Number One: Back to Bonds.

This is the most interesting possibility from a macro perspective.

AI infrastructure requires enormous amounts of capital.

And a lot of that capital doesn't come from cash sitting in a checking account.

It comes from:

corporate cash flow,

banks,

private credit,

bond markets,

equity markets,

and institutional investors.

If the demand for AI infrastructure suddenly slows...

the demand for capital also slows.

And when the economy doesn't need as much capital for investment, investors start looking for another place to put their money.

One obvious destination is:

government bonds.

Especially U.S. Treasuries.

Think about the chain reaction.

AI capex slows.

Data-center construction slows.

Demand for project financing falls.

Corporate borrowing falls.

Investors need fewer risky assets to finance.

At the same time, if weaker AI investment starts slowing economic growth...

the Federal Reserve may have more room to cut rates.

And suddenly Treasuries become much more attractive.

So ironically...

a collapse in AI capex could eventually become bullish for bonds.

This is one reason I wouldn't automatically interpret falling AI spending as purely bearish for markets.

It could be bearish for one part of the market...

while bullish for another.

Destination Number Two: AI Applications.

But there is another possibility.

The money doesn't leave AI.

It simply moves down the stack.

Today, a huge amount of capital is going into the infrastructure required to build AI.

Tomorrow, investors may start asking:

Who actually makes money from AI?

That's a very different question.

Imagine a company that spends $1 billion building AI infrastructure.

Now imagine another company that spends $100 million using AI to reduce its labor costs by $300 million every year.

Which one has the better economics?

The second company.

That's why the next phase of the AI cycle could move from:

Infrastructure

to:

Applications.

AI healthcare.

AI financial services.

AI cybersecurity.

AI enterprise software.

AI agents.

AI automation.

AI drug discovery.

AI education.

The biggest winners may not be the companies selling the GPUs.

They may be the companies using AI to completely change their cost structure.

Destination Number Three: Electricity.

Here's where the story gets complicated.

Even if AI hardware spending eventually slows...

electricity demand may not.

Why?

Because AI is not just a chip problem.

It's an energy problem.

A data center needs electricity whether the GPU inside is made by Nvidia, AMD, Google or someone else.

And if AI continues to grow, electricity infrastructure becomes increasingly valuable.

That means the capital cycle could rotate from:

chips

to:

power.

Transmission lines.

Natural gas generation.

Nuclear power.

Grid upgrades.

Transformers.

Cooling.

Energy storage.

And data-center infrastructure.

This creates an interesting scenario.

You could have:

AI chip valuations falling...

while electricity infrastructure continues to attract capital.

In other words:

The AI trade doesn't necessarily die.

It changes shape.

Destination Number Four: The Rest of the Stock Market.

This is where the second-order effect becomes really interesting.

Suppose companies suddenly stop spending hundreds of billions of dollars building AI infrastructure.

That money has to go somewhere.

Some of it could simply stay on corporate balance sheets.

Some could be returned to shareholders.

Buybacks.

Dividends.

M&A.

And some could move into completely different industries.

Imagine a world where Microsoft says:

“We're no longer going to increase AI capex by $50 billion next year.”

What happens to that money?

It doesn't disappear.

Maybe Microsoft buys another company.

Maybe it buys back shares.

Maybe it reduces debt.

Maybe it invests in cybersecurity.

Maybe it pays dividends.

And if many companies do this at the same time...

you could see a broad rotation from:

capital expenditure

toward:

shareholder returns.

That's a very different market environment.

Destination Number Five: The Next Bubble.

And this may be the most uncomfortable possibility.

Capital doesn't always move toward the most rational investment.

Sometimes it simply searches for another story.

If AI infrastructure stops delivering extraordinary returns...

investors don't suddenly become rational.

They start looking for:

the next AI.

Maybe it's robotics.

Maybe biotech.

Maybe nuclear power.

Maybe space.

Maybe crypto.

Maybe tokenized assets.

Maybe something we haven't even named yet.

This is one of the defining characteristics of bubbles.

Capital doesn't disappear.

It migrates.

But Here's the Real Problem.

There is a much more dangerous scenario.

What if AI capex doesn't simply slow down...

but collapses because the financial system can no longer support it?

That's different.

Imagine:

AI companies need more capital.

But investors become nervous.

Bond yields rise.

Credit spreads widen.

Private credit becomes more expensive.

Data-center projects get delayed.

GPU orders are canceled.

Semiconductor revenues fall.

The stocks fall.

Then the cost of capital rises even further.

And now the AI investment cycle becomes a feedback loop.

That's when an investment boom can turn into a credit cycle.

And this is the scenario I would watch most carefully.

Not:

“Is AI fake?”

But:

“Can the financial system continue funding AI at the current speed?”

The $1 Trillion Question.

So let's go back to our original question.

If $1 trillion of expected AI infrastructure spending disappears...

where does it go?

The answer is probably:

It doesn't go to one place.

It gets divided.

Some goes into Treasuries.

Some into other equities.

Some into AI applications.

Some into electricity infrastructure.

Some gets returned to shareholders.

And some chases the next speculative opportunity.

But there's something even more important.

The direction of the money may tell us where we are in the AI cycle.

Early in the cycle:

Capital flows into infrastructure.

Middle of the cycle:

Capital flows into applications.

Late in the cycle:

Capital flows into speculation.

And after the cycle:

Capital flows toward cash flow and productivity.

That's the transition I'm watching.

The Biggest AI Winner May Not Look Like an AI Company.

Here's the final twist.

Imagine that five years from now AI really does work.

Companies become dramatically more productive.

Software development becomes cheaper.

Customer service becomes automated.

Healthcare becomes more predictive.

Financial analysis becomes largely automated.

Manufacturing becomes more efficient.

What happens?

The ultimate winners may not be the companies with the most GPUs.

They may be the companies with:

the highest productivity gains.

AI becomes less of an investment theme...

and more of an economic input.

Just like electricity.

Just like computers.

Just like the internet.

And when that happens, the market will stop asking:

“How much are you spending on AI?”

It will start asking:

“How much profit does AI create?”

That is the moment the AI story becomes much bigger than Nvidia.

Final Thought.

So I don't think the most interesting question is:

“Is AI a bubble?”

Because the answer could be:

Yes.

And:

AI could still transform the economy.

Both can be true.

The more interesting question is:

What happens when the world's largest companies stop increasing AI capital expenditure at today's extraordinary rate?

If that happens, watch the money.

Watch Treasury yields.

Watch credit spreads.

Watch data-center financing.

Watch semiconductor orders.

Watch electricity investment.

And most importantly...

watch where investors start putting their money next.

Because the end of one capital cycle...

is usually the beginning of another.

The AI boom may not end with the destruction of capital.

It may end with the migration of capital.

And that migration...

could be the next trillion-dollar trade.
